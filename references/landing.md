# Landing pages

A landing, home or pricing page has a different person in front of it than an app screen: a visitor who has not
decided yet, scanning to learn what this is, whether it is for them and what to do next. Read it when the page
sells, explains or signs people up. Everything else in the skill still holds, and the template devices in
`modern.md` matter most here, because this is where they come from.

### The first screen answers three questions
- **Decision:** What must a visitor understand before they scroll?
- **Do:** What this is, who it is for, and the one thing to do next, in that order, on desktop and on a phone. The product's name and what it does are readable in five seconds; the main action is visible without scrolling.
- **Why:** Visitors decide in seconds whether to stay, and most of their attention goes to the first screen. *Source:* Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*, "The Big Bang Theory of Web Design"; Nielsen Norman Group, "Scrolling and Attention" (Fessenden, 2018).
- **Look for:** A full-height image or animation with the headline below it; a first screen that says "Build the future of work" and nothing about the product; the action hidden under a menu on the phone.
- **Unless:** A brand everyone already knows can open on a picture; a page reached from a specific link can open on that link's promise.

### The headline says what it does
- **Decision:** What does the biggest line on the page say?
- **Do:** Name what the product does for the visitor, concretely, in their words: "Turn meeting recordings into notes your team can search", not "Reimagine your finances". A slogan, if the brand has one, sits second.
- **Why:** A promotional line asks the visitor to work out what is being offered; a concrete one answers it. *Source:* Fenton & Kiefer Lee, *Nicely Said*; Krug, *Don't Make Me Think* (taglines).
- **Look for:** "The platform for modern teams"; "Unlock your potential"; a headline that would fit any product unchanged.
- **Unless:** Under the concrete line, one line of character is welcome; it is the signature, not the explanation.

### Sections in the order a visitor asks
- **Decision:** What comes after the first screen, and in what order?
- **Do:** Follow the visitor's questions: what is it, is it for me, how does it work, can I see it, who else uses it, what does it cost, what if I have a question, how do I start. Leave out a section that answers nobody's question.
- **Why:** A page read as a conversation with the visitor's questions keeps them reading; a page in the company's order loses them. *Source:* Redish, *Letting Go of the Words*.
- **Look for:** "Our mission" before what the product does; a page of six feature blocks and no price; a frequently asked questions list with questions nobody asks.
- **Unless:** A page with one job (a waitlist, an event) can be one screen long.

### Show the real product
- **Decision:** What picture explains the product?
- **Do:** A real screenshot or a short real flow of the product doing the headline's job, cropped to the part that matters, with real or clearly sample data. Before launch, a labelled sketch or a visible placeholder.
- **Why:** Seeing the product answers "how does it work" faster than any sentence, and an invented one is a claim the product cannot keep. *Source:* Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* ("above all else show the data"); `modern.md` (fake product shots).
- **Look for:** An illustrative console or chat with invented rows; a laptop mockup at an angle that makes the screen unreadable; abstract 3D shapes where the product should be.
- **Unless:** A product with no screen (an API, a service) shows what it produces: the code a developer writes, the report a customer receives.

### Features as jobs, in the form each one needs
- **Decision:** How are the features shown?
- **Do:** Each feature as what it lets the visitor do, most important first, each in the form its content needs: a screenshot for something visual, a short example for something technical, a sentence for something simple. Vary sizes by importance.
- **Why:** Three equal tiles with an icon and two lines each tell the visitor nothing is more important than anything else, and read as a template. *Source:* Redish, *Letting Go of the Words* (lead with what people want to do); `tells.md` (every section in the same bordered, shadowed card).
- **Look for:** Three identical cards with a lightning bolt, a shield and a chart; feature names that are nouns ("Analytics") instead of jobs ("Find every call where a customer asked about price").
- **Unless:** A true set of equals (three plans, three integrations of the same kind) can share one shape.

### Proof is real or absent
- **Decision:** What evidence supports the claims?
- **Do:** Only what is real and allowed: named customers who agreed, a quote with a real name and role, a number with its date and source. With none of these, leave the section out or put a visible placeholder in the draft.
- **Why:** An invented number is false information that people believe. Fake reviews and testimonials go further: they are banned outright in the US, and the UK and EU have similar bans. *Source:* US Federal Trade Commission, Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, 2024); `tells.md` (invented figures).
- **Look for:** "Trusted by 10,000+ teams" in a launch-week page; five star quotes from "Sarah K., Founder"; a logo wall of well-known companies nobody asked.
- **Unless:** Nothing; a draft names the placeholder for the owner to fill.

### Prices people can compare
- **Decision:** How is the price shown?
- **Do:** Price, currency and period on every plan, and what each includes in the same order, so plans line up. Say who each plan is for. If billing yearly costs less, show the saving in money. Mark one plan as recommended only when there is a reason.
- **Why:** Price is the information business buyers say they need most from a site, and plans that cannot be compared line by line push the choice into guesswork. *Source:* Nielsen Norman Group, "State the Price to Give B2B Sites a Competitive Advantage" (Loranger, 2013).
- **Look for:** "Contact us" as the only price; a feature listed on one plan and missing, not crossed out, on the next; "Save 20%" with no amount.
- **Unless:** A price set per contract says so and gives a starting point or the factors.

### One action, offered where the reader is ready
- **Decision:** Where does the main action appear, and how many are there?
- **Do:** One main action, with a label that says what happens ("Join the waitlist", "Start free trial"). Offer it on the first screen and again where a reader finishes a section that answers their doubt, such as after the price. A secondary path ("See how it works") stays quiet.
- **Why:** One clear next step beats several competing ones; repeating it where the reader decides saves them scrolling back. *Source:* GOV.UK Design System, "Start using a service" pattern; `hierarchy.md` (one primary action).
- **Look for:** "Get started" and "Book a demo" in the same weight; the same pill button twice in one screen; a label like "Learn more" on the main action.
- **Unless:** Two audiences with different next steps (buyers and developers) can each have one, labelled by audience.

### A form asks only what the next step needs
- **Decision:** What does the sign-up or waitlist form ask?
- **Do:** Only what the next step needs: usually just an email. Say what happens after and when ("We'll email you when your invite is ready, in March"). Confirm on the page, in place, and keep the address visible so a typo can be seen.
- **Why:** Each extra field costs completions, and a form that goes quiet after submitting leaves people unsure it worked. *Source:* Wroblewski, *Web Form Design*; `words.md` (success says what changed).
- **Look for:** Name, company, role and team size for a waitlist; a placeholder as the only label; a submit that reloads to the top with no message.
- **Unless:** A sales request can ask what the salesperson really needs to reply, and say why.

### The page holds still
- **Decision:** What moves, and how fast does the page arrive?
- **Do:** Content is readable at once and the page scrolls at the visitor's pace. Motion only shows how the product works, plays once and respects reduced motion. Images are sized and compressed so the first screen arrives quickly on a phone.
- **Why:** Hijacked scrolling disorients and frustrates people, looping motion pulls the eye from the words, and motion set off by scrolling can make people with vestibular disorders unwell. *Source:* Nielsen Norman Group, "Scrolljacking 101" (Paul, 2023); WCAG 2.2, 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (A) and 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA).
- **Look for:** Text that fades in on scroll and is invisible until it does; a hero video that autoplays with sound; a 4 MB background image.
- **Unless:** A short product demo the visitor starts is welcome.
